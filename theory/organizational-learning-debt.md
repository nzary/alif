## The idea

The ALiF Standard defines AI literacy for individuals. Organizational Learning Debt explains what happens at the level of the institution. It is the cumulative deficit between the AI-related competencies, learning infrastructure, culture and governance an organization needs for its AI portfolio and those it has actually developed, caused by repeatedly putting deployment ahead of sustained capability building.

Like technical debt in software, learning debt compounds. A gap left open in one period makes the next one larger and more expensive: staff develop workarounds, errors go unnoticed, and the next system assumes a level of competence that was never reached.

## Four dimensions

| Dimension | What accumulates |
|:---|:---|
| Capability | Shortfalls in knowledge and skills relative to the levels ALiF specifies |
| Infrastructure | Weak structures for learning: feedback loops, evaluation routines, communities of practice, protected time |
| Cultural | Norms that put throughput ahead of learning, such as celebrating deployment while discouraging people who raise concerns |
| Governance and pipeline | Deficits in AI governance and in the experiential pathways through which future experts develop, including when AI absorbs junior-level work that once built expertise |

The dimensions interact. A capability deficit produces poor feedback signals, which deepens infrastructure debt, which sustains a culture that undervalues learning. Cultural debt in turn sustains governance gaps. An organization that trains individuals while ignoring the other three dimensions will find that individual gains do not add up to organizational AI literacy.

The conceptual article sets out six testable propositions and a 16-item diagnostic, the OLDD, for measuring debt across the four dimensions.

## How it works across the ALiF layers

**Layer 1: Standard.** OLD supplies the organizational-level theory behind the Standard's informative section on organizational context, which recommends that organizations assess their learning debt when planning adoption. It also underpins the data literacy threshold: when evaluation or ethics are prioritized, data understanding and data management are developed alongside them. ALiF 2.0 proposes this as [Data Literacy First]({{ '/standard/data-literacy-first/' | relative_url }}).

**Layer 2: Measurement.** OLD generates organizational measurement constructs. The [OLD Maturity Assessment]({{ '/instruments/old-maturity-assessment/' | relative_url }}) measures how much debt exists on each dimension, the [OLD Infrastructure and Culture Audit]({{ '/instruments/old-infrastructure-culture-audit/' | relative_url }}) explains why, and the OLDD offers a short diagnostic.

**Layer 3: Programs.** Programs can record which debt dimensions they address, so an organization can plan its program portfolio against its debt profile rather than training alone.

**Layer 4: Adoption.** OLD provides a lens for adoption risk. Each dimension maps to a predictable failure pattern: staff kept at awareness level while systems assume applied competence; no feedback loops to catch near misses; learning treated as a one-time event; and governance disconnected from practice while junior development pathways erode.

## Signals to watch

ALiF suggests that adopting organizations track indirect markers of debt as part of AI governance, such as the share of AI use that is undocumented or unsupervised, incident reports relative to how widely AI is deployed, leadership time given to AI literacy as distinct from procurement, and how quickly AI policy keeps pace with new capability.
